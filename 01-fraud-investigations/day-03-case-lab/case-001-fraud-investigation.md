# Week 1 — Day 3: CASE 001 — Suspicious Transactions Investigation

## Case Information

**Case ID:** FR-001-2026  
**Case Type:** Debit Card / Transaction Fraud  
**Customer:** Michael Carter  
**Account:** Checking account ending in 4821  
**Account Age:** 4 years  
**Customer Location:** Baltimore, Maryland

> **Note:** This is a fictional training case created for portfolio development.

---

## A. Allegation

Six debit-card transactions totaling $9,900 were flagged as potentially unauthorized due to an unusual increase in spending, unfamiliar California merchants, and concentrated transaction activity between 2:04 PM and 2:39 PM.

The investigation seeks to determine whether the transactions were unauthorized or were legitimately made or authorized by the customer.

---

## B. Red Flags / Indicators

- Normal monthly spending was approximately $1,200–$1,800, while $9,900 was spent in a short period.
- Six transactions occurred within 35 minutes.
- Normal purchases primarily occurred in Maryland, while the transactions occurred in California.
- The customer had no previous transactions with the merchants involved.
- Multiple purchases occurred at the same merchant within minutes.
- Transactions occurred at both Los Angeles and Beverly Hills locations within a short period.
- The purchases were substantially outside the customer's normal spending pattern.

### Investigator Note

The transaction time period itself is not necessarily a fraud indicator. Its significance comes from the concentration, amount, merchant pattern, and departure from historical activity.

---

## C. Initial Hypotheses

### Hypothesis 1 — Unauthorized Card Use

Someone other than Michael obtained or duplicated access to the card and made the transactions without his authorization.

### Hypothesis 2 — Authorized Customer Activity

Michael personally made or authorized the transactions during his California trip.

### Additional Hypothesis — Authorized Third-Party Use

Michael authorized another person to use the card for some or all of the purchases. This possibility should be distinguished from completely unauthorized use.

---

## D. Investigative Questions

1. Does merchant surveillance footage show Michael making the purchases?
2. Do identification records corroborate that Michael made the jewelry purchase?
3. Do airline records corroborate Michael's travel to California?
4. Do hotel records corroborate his presence in Los Angeles?
5. Does available phone/location information corroborate his location during the transactions?
6. Was Michael traveling alone?
7. What exactly was purchased at each merchant?
8. Do the purchases correspond with Michael's explanation that they were wedding-related?
9. Was the same physical card used for all six transactions?
10. Did Michael possess the physical card throughout the trip?
11. Did anyone else have access to the card or know the PIN?
12. Does the merchant footage show the same individual across multiple transactions?

---

## E. Evidence Plan

| Evidence | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Merchant surveillance footage | Could corroborate who physically made the purchases. |
| Identification records | Could connect the purchaser at the jewelry store to Michael. |
| Airline/travel records | Could corroborate that Michael traveled to California. |
| Hotel check-in records | Could corroborate Michael's presence in Los Angeles. |
| Phone/location information | Could corroborate Michael's physical location during relevant transactions. |
| Detailed transaction records | Could establish transaction type, timestamps, authentication method, and other available transaction metadata. |
| Transaction authorization data | Could help determine how the transactions were authenticated and whether the same card/token was involved. |
| Purchase receipts/invoices | Could identify the merchandise purchased and test Michael's wedding-related explanation. |
| Prior transaction history | Establishes the customer's normal spending and merchant/geographic baseline. |
| Card/PIN possession information | Helps determine whether another person could have used the card or PIN. |
| California transactions immediately before the suspicious activity | Helps establish whether Michael was already physically present in the area. |

## G. Preliminary Assessment

**Assessment: Further Investigation Needed**

The available evidence does not support concluding that the transactions were fraudulent at this stage.

Evidence supporting Michael's explanation includes:

- Evidence that he traveled to California.
- California transactions occurring before the suspicious activity.
- A hotel transaction in Los Angeles.
- Physical-card transactions at the merchants.
- PIN verification on several transactions.
- Michael's statement that he authorized the purchases.

However, important questions remain unresolved, including who physically made the purchases, what was purchased, whether Michael was present, whether another person had access to the card or PIN, and whether merchant records corroborate his statement.

The appropriate disposition at this stage is continued investigation rather than an unsupported fraud or legitimate-activity conclusion.

---

## H. Most Important Evidence Gap

**Merchant surveillance footage showing the purchaser at the relevant transactions.**

This is the most important missing evidence because it could directly help answer whether Michael was physically present and making the purchases.

Footage from multiple merchants would be particularly useful for determining whether the same individual appears consistently across the transaction sequence.

---

# Final Evidence Update

Additional evidence was obtained after the initial assessment.

### Luxe Electronics Surveillance

Footage showed a person making all three Luxe Electronics purchases. The person appeared to be Michael Carter, but:

- The footage was low resolution.
- The face was partially obscured during two transactions.
- The person was wearing a baseball cap.
- The footage could not establish a definitive identification by itself.

### West Coast Jewelers Identification

The merchant reported that the purchaser presented identification containing:

- Michael Carter's name
- Correct date of birth
- Baltimore address

The store did not retain a photocopy of the identification.

### Updated Assessment

**Assessment: Further Investigation Needed**

The new evidence strengthens the legitimate-activity hypothesis but does not conclusively establish it.

Evidence supporting legitimate activity now includes:

- Travel evidence placing Michael in California.
- Hotel activity placing him in Los Angeles.
- Physical-card transactions.
- PIN verification on several transactions.
- Surveillance that appears consistent with Michael being the purchaser.
- Identification presented at the jewelry store matching Michael's identity.

Limitations remain because the surveillance footage does not provide a definitive identification and the jewelry store did not retain a copy of the identification.

The investigation therefore remains open for additional corroboration rather than being closed solely on the basis of the new evidence.

---

# Investigator Lessons

## 1. A fraud alert starts an investigation; it does not determine the conclusion.

## 2. Red flags require corroboration.

## 3. A customer statement is evidence, but should be tested against independent evidence.

## 4. Physical-card use and PIN verification are relevant evidence but do not independently prove who authorized every transaction.

## 5. Competing hypotheses help prevent confirmation bias.

## 6. Investigators should distinguish completely unauthorized use from authorized third-party use.

## 7. A strong investigative timeline reconstructs what happened, when it happened, and what evidence supports each event.

## 8. Evidence gaps should be explicitly documented rather than ignored.

## 9. New evidence should update the assessment without automatically eliminating remaining uncertainty.

## 10. The investigator follows the evidence—not the suspicion.
