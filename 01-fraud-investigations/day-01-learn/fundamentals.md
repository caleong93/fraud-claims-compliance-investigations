# Week 1 — Day 1: Fraud Investigation Fundamentals

## Objective

Build a foundation in professional fraud investigation concepts, terminology, investigative workflow, evidence evaluation, and case disposition.

## What a Fraud Investigator Does

A fraud investigator examines information suggesting fraudulent activity may have occurred and determines what the available evidence supports.

Typical activities include:

- Reviewing transactions and account activity
- Examining applications and documents
- Comparing statements and records
- Identifying patterns and anomalies
- Interviewing relevant parties
- Reviewing system and digital records
- Developing and testing investigative hypotheses
- Identifying evidence gaps
- Documenting findings
- Recommending escalation, disposition, or further investigation

## Investigation Lifecycle

**ALERT → INTAKE → TRIAGE → PREDICATION → HYPOTHESIS → EVIDENCE → ANALYSIS → FINDINGS → DISPOSITION**

### Alert

An unusual event or pattern that brings activity to someone's attention. An alert is not proof of fraud.

### Intake

The formal receipt and documentation of an allegation, referral, alert, or concern.

### Triage

The process of prioritizing a matter and determining the appropriate handling based on factors such as severity, financial exposure, customer impact, regulatory concerns, and whether activity may be ongoing.

### Predication

A reasonable factual basis that justifies opening or pursuing an investigation.

### Hypothesis

A proposed explanation that can be tested against evidence. Investigators should consider competing hypotheses rather than assuming fraud occurred.

### Evidence

Information that can support or contradict an investigative proposition or explanation.

### Analysis

The process of evaluating evidence, timelines, patterns, contradictions, relationships, and alternative explanations.

### Findings

Conclusions reached based on the evidence available.

### Disposition

The final handling or conclusion of a case. Depending on the organization, outcomes may include no fraud/no violation, unsubstantiated, inconclusive, substantiated, further investigation required, or escalation.

## Fraud Categories

### First-Party Fraud

Fraud involving a person's own identity, account, or relationship with an organization while engaging in deceptive behavior.

### Third-Party Fraud

Unauthorized use of another person's identity, account, credentials, or information.

### Internal / Employee Fraud

Fraud committed by someone inside an organization, such as an employee.

### External Fraud

Fraud committed by someone outside the organization.

### Occupational Fraud

Fraud committed by an employee against the organization where they work. Major categories include asset misappropriation, corruption, and financial statement fraud.

## Common Fraud Schemes / Typologies

- Application fraud
- Account takeover
- Transaction fraud
- Identity fraud
- Expense fraud
- Procurement/vendor fraud
- Claims fraud

## Red Flags

A red flag is an indicator that activity may be suspicious or unusual and warrants further examination.

Examples:

- Inconsistent statements
- Unusual transaction timing
- Rapid transactions
- Unusual geographic activity
- Repeated claims
- Recently changed account information
- Unusual device or login activity
- Missing or altered documentation
- Unusual relationships between parties
- Activity inconsistent with historical behavior

**Key principle:** A red flag tells an investigator what to investigate; it does not determine the conclusion.

## Red Flag vs. Evidence

**Red flag:** Four high-value purchases occur in California within 45 minutes even though the customer historically makes small purchases in Maryland.

**Investigative question:** Did the customer authorize the transactions, or were they conducted by an unauthorized party?

**Potential evidence:**

- Customer location
- Transaction history
- IP information
- Device information
- Authentication records
- Login history
- Customer statement

## Hypothesis-Based Investigation

Investigators should test multiple plausible explanations.

Example hypotheses for suspicious transactions:

- The transactions were unauthorized.
- The customer authorized the transactions.
- The activity was legitimate but unusual.
- The account credentials were compromised without the customer's knowledge.

The investigation should identify evidence that supports or contradicts each hypothesis.

## Evidence Gaps

An evidence gap is important information that is currently unavailable but is needed to answer an investigative question.

Examples:

- Whether the customer was actually in the transaction location
- Who accessed an account
- Whether authentication was successful
- Whether another person had authorized access
- Whether a transaction was later reversed

**Key principle:** Access does not establish responsibility.

## Escalation

Investigations may require escalation to specialized or decision-making functions such as:

- Senior investigators
- Management
- Legal
- Compliance
- Special Investigations Unit (SIU)
- AML / Financial Crimes
- Human Resources
- Law enforcement or regulatory teams, where appropriate

## Disposition Concepts

Terminology varies by organization, but investigators may encounter outcomes such as:

- No fraud / no violation
- Unsubstantiated
- Inconclusive
- Further investigation required
- Substantiated
- Escalated

**Inconclusive** means the available evidence is insufficient to determine what happened or whether the allegation is supported.

## Core Investigator Principles

1. Suspicion is not proof.
2. Access does not establish responsibility.
3. A red flag does not automatically mean fraud or misconduct.
4. Investigators should test competing hypotheses.
5. Innocent explanations must be considered.
6. Evidence should be connected to the investigative question it addresses.
7. Contradictory and missing evidence should be documented.
8. Findings should match the strength of the evidence.
9. When evidence is insufficient, an inconclusive or further-investigation conclusion can be appropriate.
10. **The investigator follows the evidence—not the suspicion.**

## Applied Exercise

### Scenario

A customer has had an account for three years. Historically, they make small purchases in Maryland. Yesterday, the account suddenly had four high-value purchases totaling $8,700 in California within 45 minutes. The customer has never previously made purchases in California.

**Red flag:** Four high-value purchases in California within 45 minutes, inconsistent with the customer's historical activity.

**Investigative question:** Did the customer authorize the transactions, or were they conducted by an unauthorized party?

**Evidence identified:**

1. Customer location
2. Transaction history
3. IP information

This exercise demonstrates the **red flag → investigative question → evidence** framework.
